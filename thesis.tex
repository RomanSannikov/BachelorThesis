\chapter{Introduction}
Over the past four decades, there was a major leap in robotics, especially in the field of
quadrupeds. Many great papers developing the domain were published leading to significant
advancements. The jump was from early days one-legged hopping machines to a four-legged "trotting"
robots able to traverse through difficult terrain and obstacles, and even autonomous navigation in
unstructured environments. Legged locomotion showed that it is one of the most versatile forms of
mobility. But most of the research was done in labs, requiring substantial resources for prototyping
and pushing the boundaries of the domain, which creates a gap between lab research and
consumer-level accessibility.

The objective of this thesis is to address that gap by designing, constructing, and controlling
a quadruped robot as an integrated project. The work covers the full development pipeline:
mechanical design using modern CAD tools and 3D printing, selection and characterization of
actuators, implementation of statically stable gaits based on established stability criteria,
development of a control system, integration of computer vision, and validation through physical
experiments. By following this end-to-end process, the thesis demonstrates how classical locomotion
and computer vision principles can be realized on a budget-friendly platform.

A deliberate choice was made to use low-cost, commercially available components and
rapid-prototyping techniques wherever possible. This decision was not driven by cost
reduction alone; rather, it ensures that the resulting platform is reproducible.
In this paper, I would like to review the research and practical achievements that have been done
throughout the years and combine it in a project, the cost of which will be less. The idea is to
establish a base for low-cost quadruped robots to make them more affordable for day-to-day use in
different fields of human lives.

\section{Literature review}
\label{sec:literature-review}
There is a number of "classical" papers that can be used to lay the foundation for this project.
They vary from gaits and control to computer vision and successful real-world examples.

The most important thing for a quadruped robot is locomotion. The mathematical analysis in the 1968
"On the stability properties of quadruped creeping gaits" Mathematical Biosciences journal article
shows, that there is "a unique optimum gait that maximizes static stability". The work explains
that for static stability the center of gravity must be within the support polygon. This shape
is formed by connecting all feet that touch the ground (contact points). Though, stability can be
achived by dynamic means as well. The concept of dynamic stability is discussed in the 1984
"Experiments in Balance with a 3D One-Legged Hopping Machine", which explains the control
algorithms: forward velocity, body altitude, and hopping height. In the recent years, compute
became more accessible and the development of neural networks allowed to creation of simulated
environments, in which ditigal versions of robots were put in and given a task to arrive from
point A to B without being programmed to do so using Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL). This
approach was used in 2019 "Learning Agile and Dynamic Motor Skills for Legged Robots" for ANYmal
robot. The researchers applied the real-world behavior of the robot's motors to the simulation
that allowed the simulation to be more realistic and helped the robot learn imperfect nuances of
the world.

While the theoretical foundations establish how quadruped locomotion works, several landmark systems
demonstrate what is achievable with mature engineering. Boston Dynamics' BigDog (2008) was the first
to demonstrate load-carrying dynamic locomotion on rough terrain, proving quadrupeds could operate
in real-world conditions outside the lab. It was a direct descendant of the 1984 hopping machine
experiments and it made evident that dynamic stability is the key to traversing challenging terrain.
Similarly, MIT's Cheetah 3 (2018) showed that model-based control could achieve robust blind
locomotion over obstacles. These systems represented the state-of-the-art capability of quadruped
robots at their time. The projects were sponsored by DARPA and MIT respectively and did not focus
on cost reduction, but rather on pushing the boundaries of what quadruped robots could do.

Another key component of the project is computer vision. Generally, robots can be controlled by a
human operator, which remains a very practical approach. However, computer vision is a significant
step towards autonomy and high-level task execution. Vision is critical for navigation, obstacle
avoidance, and interaction with the environment. For this project, I decided to use YOLO,
a state-of-the-art object detection model, based on the "You Only Look Once" (2016) architecture,
which was a game-changer in the field of real-time object detection. The model divides the image
into a grid, and each grid cell is responsible for predicting bounding boxes, confidence score, and
class probabilities of an object whose center falls into that cell. As described in the paper:
``[The] network architecture is inspired by the GoogLeNet model for image classification\ldots\ [It]
has 24 convolutional layers followed by 2 fully connected layers. Instead of the inception modules
used by GoogLeNet,\ldots [it simply uses] $1 \times 1$ reduction layers followed by $3 \times 3$
convolutional layers\ldots''. The original YOLO uses the Sum-Squared Error (SSE) loss function
between the predictions and the ground truth. While the architecture has evolved significantly in
subsequent versions to utilize CSPDarknet, a high-performance convolutional neural network backbone,
and advanced loss functions, the fundamental principle of grid-based single-pass detection remains
the core of the model used in this project. Additionally, it is worth mentioning Object Detection
with Discriminatively Trained Part Based Models (2010), which is a classic paper on training models
to recognize objects based on their parts, because quadruped robots usually have limited field of
view due to their height. Applying the logic of part-based modeling to train a model to recognize
an object by its lower part is crucial for the robot to "understand" the environment.

While the architecture and approach to train the model are important, the datasets used for training
enable the model to reach its full potential. In this project, I utilize two of the most
significant benchmarks in the history of object detection: Common Objects in Context (COCO) (2014)
and Stanford Dogs (2011). The COCO dataset established the standard for detecting objects in
"natural" settings rather than perfectly centered studio shots. It contains over 330K images, with
more than 2.5 million labeled instances across 91 object categories, including humans, dogs, cats,
etc., easily recognizable by a 4-year-old. The Stanford Dogs dataset ``\ldots includes over 22,000
annotated images of dogs belonging to 120 species. Each image is annotated with a bounding box and
object class label.''

\chapter{Methodology}
\section{Methods and technologies used}
Developing a functional quadruped robot on a limited budget requires navigating a wide landscape
of hardware components, software frameworks, and design methodologies. While the robotics field
offers a rich variety of actuators, computing platforms, sensors, and control strategies, the
project constraints significantly narrow the range of viable options. Cost, reproducibility, and
the mechanical demands of legged locomotion impose trade-offs at every stage of the design
process, forcing a careful balance between performance, affordability, and ease of integration.
The following subsections outline the requirements that shaped this design space and present the
technologies and models selected to address them.

\subsection{Project requirements}
\label{subsec:project-requirements}
There are quite a lot of requirements and limitations in this project as it seeks to demonstrate
a way to develop a low-cost quadruped robot. There are three big groups of limitations that define
requirements. The first, and most significant, is cost. It influences the other group which is
the hardware. The hardware, limited by cost, in turn constrains the software and algorithms. All
together they create a really tight framework for the project, which is a good thing since it
forces to creativity and finding new solutions.

My requirements for the project are the following:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The robot should be able to walk on flat surfaces in all directions, i.e. forward,
backwards, sideways, and turning in place. Flat surfaces were chosen here because rough terrain
is too complex for the hardware the project uses.
    \item The robot should have 12 degrees of freedom (DOF) --- where each joint that can move
independently contributes one DOF --- to allow for a variety of gaits and maneuvers. Each leg
should have 3 DOF: hip abduction/adduction (side-to-side movement), hip flexion/extension
(forward-backward movement), and knee flexion/extension.
    \item It should have computer vision capabilities to detect and recognize objects. For the
sake of demonstration, I chose dogs and humans as the target objects because they represent the
primary agents (humans) and subjects (dogs) the robot is expected to encounter and interact with.
	\item The robot should be easy to assemble and reproduce by others. The components should
be available, affordable, and designed with swappability in mind to allow for future upgrades and/or
repairs.
\end{itemize}

On the limitation side, the cost of the whole project should not exceed 300 EUR, which is quite
limiting for the result the project wants to achieve.

The hardware and software choices are affected by this limitation and the technology selection
will be discussed in the next section.
\subsection{Technologies selection}
\label{subsec:technology-selection}
Everything starts with the model. Nowadays, 3D printers are quite accessible for people making them
a great choice for the project since it allows producing custom elements perfectly fitting and
satisfying the requirements and at the same time being easily reproducible by the others. At my
disposal were Bambu Lab printers A1 mini, X1C Carbon and H2D series. I tried them all and settled
on the last one since it has two nozzles, allowing the use of two types of filament at the same
time: PLA and PETG. PLA is a great general material for the robot itself, while PETG was used for
supporting elements. It is possible to use only PLA, and I have tried that, but PETG makes life
easier since it does not stick to PLA, making it a perfect choice for supports. The design of the
robot was done in Onshape, a free cloud-based CAD web software with enough functionality for the
project. I have not created the initial model by myself, as it would take much more time, but rather
found a suitable one with Creative Commons (4.0 International License) on the Internet made
by Tom Knox and modified it to fit the requirements. The requirements not met by the initial model
were 12 DOF and ease of assembly. What the original model did provide was a solid base and design I
wanted, 3 DOF per front leg, and a place for the camera module.

What made the original 3D model hard to assemble was the fact that it consisted of 149 unique parts
connected by different types of screws. The most problematic part was the leg. All four legs are the
same just mirrored, which made redesigning much easier. To reduce complexity of assembly, I
redesigned legs and the body to be print-in-place, meaning ready to use parts without the need of
additional assembly. I also reduced the number of unique screws in the project to just 1 type, which
is M3 of two sizes.

For the actuators, I chose WS-MG996R digital servos with metal gears, which are widely used in hobby
robotics, 3 per leg, which gives 12 DOF in total. These servos are really affordable and deliver
\(11 \text{ kg}\cdot\text{cm}\) stall torque with a speed of 0.17--0.14 seconds per 60°, operating
at 4.8V to 7.2V DC. On the other hand, cheaper actuators mean low precision, feedback, and torque,
which are necessary for this type of project. Considering all pros and cons, these are the bare
minimum for the project.

For the main controller board, I chose Raspberry Pi 5 4GB. It is a nice little computer powerful
enough for controlling servos and running small object recognition models. A big advantage of
this computer is that it runs Raspberry Pi OS, which is a Linux based operating system, allowing
fast prototyping and running existing programs and tools. The Raspberry Pi 5 offers significant
improvements over its predecessor, including faster GPIO and better overall performance, making it
well-suited for this application. The Raspberry Pi does not have enough GPIO pins to control all
12 servos, so I went with PCA9685, a 16-channel, 12-bit PWM (Pulse Width Modulation) driver chip.
It uses I2C communication protocol and takes just 4 Raspberry Pi GPIO pins: VCC 3V, SDA, SCL, and
GND. The servos consumption can spike up to 2.5A per servo, so in total it is 30A, which is way more
than the Raspberry Pi can provide. To solve this problem, I use an external power supply, which is
4 18650 2500 mAh Lithium-ion batteries able to produce enough current for the servos. Connected in
a 2S2P configuration (two series pairs in parallel), they provide 7.4V with a total capacity of
5000 mAh. With that the potential difference is larger than what the servos and Raspberry Pi can
handle, so I use buck converters to step down the voltage to 7V for the servos and 5V for
the Raspberry Pi.

For the computer vision part, I use a 5-megapixel Raspberry Pi Camera Module v1.3, which features
an OmniVision OV5647 Color CMOS sensor. The camera has a 3.6 mm focal length lens with a
$54^\circ \times 41^\circ$
angle of view, providing a horizontal field of view roughly equal to the distance from the subject
at typical operating ranges. The camera uses a CSI ribbon cable to connect to the high-speed,
dual-purpose MIPI port on the Raspberry Pi, offering lower latency and CPU overhead compared to
USB cameras.

On the software side, I use Python as the main programming language. It is a great choice for rapid
prototyping and has a huge ecosystem of libraries for robotics, computer vision, and machine
learning. For controlling the servos, I use the Adafruit PCA9685 library, which provides a simple
interface for sending PWM (Pulse Width Modulation) signals to the servos. PWM varies the duty
cycle of a square wave to control the average voltage delivered, with pulse widths typically
ranging from 1 to 2 milliseconds corresponding to 0 to 180 degrees of servo rotation. For object
detection, I use the Ultralytics YOLO library, which offers a streamlined interface for YOLO-based
inference and integrates well with the Raspberry Pi platform.
