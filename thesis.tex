\chapter{Introduction}
Over the past four decades, there was a major leap in robotics, especially in the field of
quadrupeds. Many great papers developing the domain were published leading to significant
advancements. The jump was from early days one-legged hopping machines to a four-legged "trotting"
robots able to traverse through difficult terrain and obstacles, and even autonomous navigation in
unstructured environments. Legged locomotion showed that it is one of the most versatile forms of
mobility. But most of the research was done in labs, requiring substantial resources for prototyping
and pushing the boundaries of the domain, which creates a gap between lab research and
consumer-level accessibility.

The objective of this thesis is to address that gap by designing, constructing, and controlling
a quadruped robot as an integrated project. The work covers the full development pipeline:
mechanical design using modern CAD tools and 3D printing, selection and characterization of
actuators, implementation of statically stable gaits based on established stability criteria,
development of a control system, integration of computer vision, and validation through physical
experiments. By following this end-to-end process, the thesis demonstrates how classical locomotion
and computer vision principles can be realized on a budget-friendly platform.

A deliberate choice was made to use low-cost, commercially available components and
rapid-prototyping techniques wherever possible. This decision was not driven by cost
reduction alone; rather, it ensures that the resulting platform is reproducible.
In this paper, I would like to review the research and practical achievements that have been done
throughout the years and combine it in a project, the cost of which will be less. The idea is to
establish a base for low-cost quadruped robots to make them more affordable for day-to-day use in
different fields of human lives.

\section{Literature review}
\label{sec:literature-review}
There is a number of "classical" papers that can be used to lay the foundation for this project.
They vary from gaits and control to computer vision and successful real-world examples.

The most important thing for a quadruped robot is locomotion. The mathematical analysis in the 1968
"On the stability properties of quadruped creeping gaits" Mathematical Biosciences journal article
shows, that there is "a unique optimum gait that maximizes static stability". The work explains
that for static stability the center of gravity must be within the support polygon. This shape
is formed by connecting all feet that touch the ground (contact points). Though, stability can be
achieved by dynamic means as well. The concept of dynamic stability is discussed in the 1984
"Experiments in Balance with a 3D One-Legged Hopping Machine", which explains the control
algorithms: forward velocity, body altitude, and hopping height. In the recent years, compute
became more accessible and the development of neural networks allowed to creation of simulated
environments, in which digital versions of robots were put in and given a task to arrive from
point A to B without being programmed to do so using Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL). This
approach was used in 2019 "Learning Agile and Dynamic Motor Skills for Legged Robots" for ANYmal
robot. The researchers applied the real-world behavior of the robot's motors to the simulation
that allowed the simulation to be more realistic and helped the robot learn imperfect nuances of
the world.

While the theoretical foundations establish how quadruped locomotion works, several landmark systems
demonstrate what is achievable with mature engineering. Boston Dynamics' BigDog (2008) was the first
to demonstrate load-carrying dynamic locomotion on rough terrain, proving quadrupeds could operate
in real-world conditions outside the lab. It was a direct descendant of the 1984 hopping machine
experiments and it made evident that dynamic stability is the key to traversing challenging terrain.
Similarly, MIT's Cheetah 3 (2018) showed that model-based control could achieve robust blind
locomotion over obstacles. These systems represented the state-of-the-art capability of quadruped
robots at their time. The projects were sponsored by DARPA and MIT respectively and did not focus
on cost reduction, but rather on pushing the boundaries of what quadruped robots could do.

Another key component of the project is computer vision. Generally, robots can be controlled by a
human operator, which remains a very practical approach. However, computer vision is a significant
step towards autonomy and high-level task execution. Vision is critical for navigation, obstacle
avoidance, and interaction with the environment. For this project, I decided to use YOLO,
a state-of-the-art object detection model, based on the "You Only Look Once" (2016) architecture,
which was a game-changer in the field of real-time object detection. The model divides the image
into a grid, and each grid cell is responsible for predicting bounding boxes, confidence score, and
class probabilities of an object whose center falls into that cell. As described in the paper:
``[The] network architecture is inspired by the GoogLeNet model for image classification\ldots\ [It]
has 24 convolutional layers followed by 2 fully connected layers. Instead of the inception modules
used by GoogLeNet,\ldots [it simply uses] $1 \times 1$ reduction layers followed by $3 \times 3$
convolutional layers\ldots''. The original YOLO uses the Sum-Squared Error (SSE) loss function
between the predictions and the ground truth. While the architecture has evolved significantly in
subsequent versions to utilize CSPDarknet, a high-performance convolutional neural network backbone,
and advanced loss functions, the fundamental principle of grid-based single-pass detection remains
the core of the model used in this project. Additionally, it is worth mentioning Object Detection
with Discriminatively Trained Part Based Models (2010), which is a classic paper on training models
to recognize objects based on their parts, because quadruped robots usually have limited field of
view due to their height. Applying the logic of part-based modeling to train a model to recognize
an object by its lower part is crucial for the robot to "understand" the environment.

While the architecture and approach to train the model are important, the datasets used for training
enable the model to reach its full potential. In this project, I utilize two of the most
significant benchmarks in the history of object detection: Common Objects in Context (COCO) (2014)
and Stanford Dogs (2011). The COCO dataset established the standard for detecting objects in
"natural" settings rather than perfectly centered studio shots. It contains over $330,000$ images,
with more than $2.5$ million labeled instances across 91 object categories, including humans, dogs,
cats, etc., easily recognizable by a 4-year-old. The Stanford Dogs dataset ``\ldots includes over
$22,000$ annotated images of dogs belonging to 120 species. Each image is annotated with a bounding
box and object class label.''

\chapter{Methodology}
\section{Methods and technologies used}
Developing a functional quadruped robot on a limited budget requires navigating a wide landscape
of hardware components, software frameworks, and design methodologies. While the robotics field
offers a rich variety of actuators, computing platforms, sensors, and control strategies, the
project constraints significantly narrow the range of viable options. Cost, reproducibility, and
the mechanical demands of legged locomotion impose trade-offs at every stage of the design
process, forcing a careful balance between performance, affordability, and ease of integration.
The following subsections outline the requirements that shaped this design space and present the
technologies and models selected to address them.

\subsection{Project requirements}
\label{subsec:project-requirements}
There are quite a lot of requirements and limitations in this project as it seeks to demonstrate
a way to develop a low-cost quadruped robot. There are three big groups of limitations that define
requirements. The first, and most significant, is cost. It influences the other group which is
the hardware. The hardware, limited by cost, in turn constrains the software and algorithms. All
together they create a really tight framework for the project, which is a good thing since it
forces to creativity and finding new solutions.

My requirements for the project are the following:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The robot should be able to walk on flat surfaces in all directions, i.e. forward,
backwards, sideways, and turning in place. Flat surfaces were chosen here because rough terrain
is too complex for the hardware the project uses.
    \item The robot should have 12 degrees of freedom (DOF), where each joint that can move
independently contributes one DOF, to allow for a variety of gaits and maneuvers. Each leg
should have 3 DOF: hip abduction and adduction (side-to-side movement), hip flexion and extension
(forward-backward movement), and knee flexion and extension.
    \item It should have computer vision capabilities to detect and recognize objects. For the
sake of demonstration, I chose dogs and humans as the target objects because they represent the
primary agents (humans) and subjects (dogs) the robot is expected to encounter and interact with.
	\item The robot should be easy to assemble and reproduce by others. The components should
be available, affordable, and designed with swappability in mind to allow for future upgrades and/or
repairs.
\end{itemize}

On the limitation side, the cost of the whole project should not exceed 300 EUR, which is quite
limiting for the result the project wants to achieve.

The hardware and software choices are affected by this limitation and the technology selection
will be discussed in the next section.
\subsection{Technologies selection}
\label{subsec:technology-selection}
Everything starts with the model. Nowadays, 3D printers are quite accessible for people making them
a great choice for the project since it allows producing custom elements perfectly fitting and
satisfying the requirements and at the same time being easily reproducible by the others. At my
disposal were Bambu Lab printers A1 mini, X1C Carbon and H2D series. I tried them all and settled
on the last one since it has two nozzles, allowing the use of two types of filament at the same
time: PLA and PETG. PLA is a great general material for the robot itself, while PETG was used for
supporting elements. It is possible to use only PLA, and I have tried that, but PETG makes life
easier since it does not stick to PLA, making it a perfect choice for supports. The design of the
robot was done in Onshape, a free cloud-based CAD web software with enough functionality for the
project. For the slicer, which is software that converts 3D models into G-code instructions for the
printer, specifying toolpaths, layer heights, and print parameters, Bambu Lab's Bambu Studio was
used as it perfectly works with the printers and has the sufficient functionality for the project.
I have not created the initial model by myself, as it would take much more time, but rather found
a suitable one with Creative Commons (4.0 International License) on the Internet made by Tom Knox
and modified it to fit the requirements. The requirements not met by the initial model were 12 DOF
and ease of assembly. What the original model did provide was a solid base and design I wanted,
3 DOF per front leg, and a place for the camera module.

What made the original 3D model hard to assemble was the fact that it consisted of 149 unique parts
connected by different types of screws. The most problematic part was the leg. All four legs are the
same just mirrored, which made redesigning much easier. To reduce complexity of assembly, I
redesigned legs and the body to be print-in-place, meaning ready to use parts without the need of
additional assembly. I also reduced the number of unique screws in the project to just 1 type, which
is M3 of two sizes.

For the actuators, I chose WS-MG996R digital servos with metal gears, which are widely used in hobby
robotics, 3 per leg, which gives 12 DOF in total. These servos are really affordable and deliver
\(11 \text{ kg}\cdot\text{cm}\) stall torque with a speed of 0.17--0.14 seconds per 60°, operating
at 4.8V to 7.2V DC. On the other hand, cheaper actuators mean low precision, feedback, and torque,
which are necessary for this type of project. Considering all pros and cons, these are the bare
minimum for the project.

For the main controller board, I chose Raspberry Pi 4 4GB. It is a nice little computer powerful
enough for controlling servos and running small object recognition models with the following
characteristics: a 1.5GHz 64-bit Broadcom BCM2711 Quad-core Cortex-A72 (ARM v8) processor with
on-chip VideoCore VI GPU, supporting OpenGL ES 3.1 and Vulkan 1.2, Dual-band 2.4 GHz and 5.0 GHz
IEEE 802.11ac wireless LAN (Wi-Fi 5) and Bluetooth 5.0 (BLE), a Gigabit Ethernet port, two USB 3.0
ports and two USB 2.0 ports, a PCIe 2.0 x1 interface, 40 pin GPIO header, two 2-lane MIPI
camera/display transceivers, a USB-C power supply port, and 4GB LPDDR4-3200 SDRAM. A big advantage
of this computer is that it runs Raspberry Pi OS, which is a Linux based operating system, allowing
fast prototyping and running existing programs and tools. The Raspberry Pi 4 offers significant
improvements over its predecessor, including faster GPIO and better overall performance, making it
well-suited for this application. The Raspberry Pi does not have enough GPIO pins to control all
12 servos, so I went with PCA9685, a 16-channel, 12-bit PWM (Pulse Width Modulation) driver chip.
It uses I2C communication protocol and takes just 4 Raspberry Pi GPIO pins: VCC 3.3V, SDA, SCL, and
GND. The servos consumption can spike up to 2.5A per servo, so in total it is 30A, which is way more
than the Raspberry Pi can provide. To solve this problem, I decided to use an external power supply,
which is 4 18650 2500 mAh Lithium-ion batteries able to produce enough current for the servos.
Connected in a 2S2P configuration (two series pairs in parallel), they provide 7.4V with a total
capacity of 5000 mAh. With that the potential difference is larger than what the servos and
Raspberry Pi can handle, so I use buck converters to step down the voltage to 7V for the servos and
5V for the Raspberry Pi.

For the computer vision part, I use a 5-megapixel Raspberry Pi Camera Module v1.3, which features
an OmniVision OV5647 Color CMOS sensor. The camera has a 3.6 mm focal length lens with a
$54^\circ \times 41^\circ$
angle of view, providing a horizontal field of view roughly equal to the distance from the subject
at typical operating ranges. The camera uses a CSI ribbon cable to connect to the high-speed,
dual-purpose MIPI port on the Raspberry Pi, offering lower latency and CPU overhead compared to
USB cameras.

On the software side, I use Python as the main programming language. It is a great choice for rapid
prototyping and has a huge ecosystem of libraries for robotics, computer vision, and machine
learning. For controlling the servos, I use the Adafruit PCA9685 library, which provides a simple
interface for sending PWM (Pulse Width Modulation) signals to the servos. PWM varies the duty
cycle of a square wave to control the average voltage delivered, with pulse widths typically
ranging from 1 to 2 milliseconds corresponding to 0 to 180 degrees of servo rotation. For object
detection, I use the Ultralytics YOLO library, which offers a streamlined interface for YOLO-based
inference and integrates well with the Raspberry Pi platform.
\subsection{Implementation}
\label{subsec:implementation}
The implementation started by downloading the original model and printing the most important
part --- the leg. The leg model consisted of 7 parts, which took the printer around 3 hours. After,
I assembled the leg, attached the hip to a servo and connected the servo directly to the Raspberry
Pi with jumper wires. Printing it and testing that the leg works was a crucial step that helped me
to verify that the model is capable of moving and having the physical model helped me understand
what needed to be changed. Later, I started redesigning the leg to be print-in-place. First of all,
it is important to understand the principles of such design. The most important idea is that there
should be no overhanging parts, meaning that all the parts should be supported by the print itself.
To achieve that, the maximum overhang angle should not exceed 45 degrees, beyond which support
material would be required. Another thing to keep in mind is the clearance between the moving parts.
Through experimentation, I found that 0.65 mm provides the best balance between smooth motion and
minimal play, so this value was used for most clearances in the model. The height of every print was
standard, 0.2 mm, and the support parameter was set for hanging parts like curves of the legs and
body "ribs". The final leg consists of 6 elements: motor extension, extension link, shoulder disk,
femur, secondary link, and tibia, printed as one module. After removing it from the printer bed and 
detaching the supports, it is movable and ready to be installed. To get to the final design, I went
through around twenty iterations of the leg and a couple of iterations of the body. Several design
challenges emerged during these iterations and to achieve the requirements while using the same
design, I had to break the basic principles. For example, a support blocker is used for the leg to
block the support from being generated in the motor extension part, creating a bridging surface that
spans a gap without support. Another example, showing the challenge of designing print-in-place
models, is the knee. The knee joint employs a cylindrical pivot pin that is integrated into the
tibia (lower leg), with the femur (upper leg) captured between the two halves of the tibia joint,
forming a sandwich-like structure. This arrangement creates overhangs on both the top and bottom
surfaces of the femur where it interfaces with the tibia, requiring support material to be generated
in two places: between the upper tibia surface and the femur, and between the femur and the lower
tibia surface. The overhang surface is large and cannot be recovered if drooped and melted to the
other part, thus support was necessary, so unfortunately, each knee has a bit of PETG support in it.
PLA filament can also be used as an alternative to PETG for support, requiring breaking it by
moving the knee joint after printing. Beyond the main structural parts, the model contains eight
servo holders that are printed separately. Each holder weighs 5 grams and does not require support
material. The holders attach to the servos with screws, while the connection to the body uses
integrated pins that slot into corresponding holes, eliminating the need for additional fasteners.
In total, printing the complete model requires approximately 20.5 hours and 490 grams of filament.
The body takes 7 hours and 154 grams, each of the four legs requires 3 hours and 74 grams, and the
eight servo holders together take 1.5 hours and 40 grams.

When the whole model was stable and ready, I started assembling it all together. With the experience
I gained, it would take around one hour to assemble everything. Connecting PCA9685 to the Raspberry
Pi was a straightforward process. The control pin connection layout is the following: VCC to 3.3V,
GND to GND, SDA to GPIO 2 (SDA), and SCL to GPIO 3 (SCL). PCA9685 does not power the servos from
the controller, instead there is a separate V+ and GND terminals. Those are connected to an
external power supply. While testing I used a lab power supply, set to 6V and 6A, which was enough
for testing purposes. In order to enable I2C communication interface on the Raspberry Pi, it needed
to be enabled through the Raspberry Pi Configuration tool, which can be accessed through
\texttt{sudo raspi-config} under Interface Options, I2C. After that, I2C utilities should be
installed by running \texttt{sudo apt install i2c-tools}. The connectivity can be verified by
running \texttt{i2cdetect -y 1}, which should show the PCA9685 at address $0x40$ (hexadecimal). The
address is hardcoded to $0x40$ by the manufacturer, as the default base address for this chip. In
the I2C protocol, each device requires a unique 7-bit address for the master controller to identify
it. The PCA9685 uses a partially configurable address scheme where the upper bits are fixed
internally (binary $1000000$, corresponding to hexadecimal $0x40$), while the lower six bits can be
modified via external address pins labeled $A^0$ through $A^5$. This design allows up to 62 PCA9685
devices to coexist on the same bus by changing the pin configurations, enabling expansion of PWM
outputs without requiring additional communication channels. For instance, setting pin $A^0$ high
while keeping others low changes the address from $0x40$ to $0x41$. This flexible addressing makes
the PCA9685 well-suited for robotics applications requiring numerous servo motors. For this project,
I am not changing the default address, so it remains at $0x40$. The other pins are 16 channels for
PWM output, which are connected to the servos. Each channel consists of three pins: V+ (power),
GND (ground), and OUT (signal). I grouped them by leg in this order: hip extension, knee flexion,
and hip abduction, so it is easier to understand which channel controls which joint. The PCA9685
board exposes sockets for the servos, so it is convenient to connect them without soldering,
however, the board itself exposes the I2C pins, while Raspberry Pi exposes its GPIO pins, so I had
to solder wires to connect them together. Each servo has $180^\circ$ range of motion, so before
attaching servos to the robot, I set: hip to $90^\circ$, knee to $60^\circ$, and abduction to
$95^\circ$, to their home position. These values are hardcoded in the code and all movements are
relative to them. Then, the servos are attached as following: the hip abduction servo is screwed
vertically to its slot in the body and a dual-arm horn is attached to the gear, after that the hip
extension and knee flexion servos are put together and secured with servo holders on the sides, then
another dual-arm horn is attached to the knee flexion servo, while a round disc horn is screwed to
the back side of the femur. Ultimately, the horns are connected to the hip and motor extension
respectively. The same procedure is repeated for all four legs. After that, the electronics are
installed inside the body and the Raspberry Pi camera module is mounted to the front camera slot.
With the mechanical assembly complete, the control software can command movement.

A step consists of two phases: the swing phase, where the leg is parabolically lifted and moved
forward, and the stance phase, where the foot drives backward relative to the chassis, generating
the ground reaction force that pushes the robot's body forward. A gait is the periodic pattern of
these foot movements. To translate desired foot positions into servo angles, the system uses a
lookup table approach rather than inverse kinematics. This method was chosen because the mechanical
coupling in the leg design, where hip movement affects knee angle, makes standard analytical
solutions impractical. The lookup table contains pre-calibrated mappings between joint angles and
foot positions, created by setting different servo angles and measuring the resulting foot
positions. With the hip mounting point defined as the coordinate origin, the foot position is
expressed in millimeters relative to this reference. The calibration data consists of 81
measurements spanning the feasible range of hip and knee angles. Each entry records the hip angle,
knee angle, and the corresponding foot coordinates, capturing the reachable workspace of the leg.
When the control system requests a target foot position $(x_t, y_t)$, it locates the nearest
calibration points in the two-dimensional position space and interpolates between them using inverse
distance weighting. For each calibration point $i$ with foot position $(x_i, y_i)$, the Euclidean
distance to the target is computed as
\begin{equation}
d_i = \sqrt{(x_t - x_i)^2 + (y_t - y_i)^2}.
\end{equation}
The four nearest points are selected, and a weight is assigned to each according to the inverse
square of its distance:
\begin{equation}
w_i = \frac{1}{d_i^{\,2}}.
\end{equation}
These weights are normalized so that they sum to unity,
\begin{equation}
\hat{w}_i = \frac{w_i}{\sum_{j=1}^{4} w_j},
\end{equation}
and the interpolated joint angles are obtained as the weighted average of the corresponding
calibration angles:
\begin{equation}
\theta_{\text{hip}} = \sum_{i=1}^{4} \hat{w}_i \, \theta_{\text{hip},i}, \qquad
\theta_{\text{knee}} = \sum_{i=1}^{4} \hat{w}_i \, \theta_{\text{knee},i}.
\end{equation}

In practice, the implementation includes several practical adjustments: if the target lies within
$0.1$~mm of a calibration point, that point is returned directly without interpolation; distances
below $0.001$~mm are clamped to prevent division by zero; and the final interpolated angles are
rounded to the nearest integer, since the servos accept only whole-degree commands. This method
assigns greater influence to closer calibration points, producing smooth angle estimates even for
positions that were not explicitly measured. The approach is particularly suitable for this
mechanism because the mechanical coupling between the hip and knee joints, arising from the
linkage geometry of the leg, precludes a closed-form analytical solution. While inverse
kinematics is the conventional approach in robotics literature, empirical mapping provides a
practical alternative when mechanical complexity or manufacturing tolerances make analytical models
unreliable. The abduction joints, which control side-to-side leg movement, are not included in the
lookup table because their motion is kinematically decoupled from the primary leg swing mechanism.
These joints operate independently and are controlled through direct angle commands. Each servo is
individually calibrated to account for mechanical variations and assembly differences between left
and right legs. A direction multiplier ensures that positive commands produce consistent physical
movements across all legs, while center offsets compensate for variations in the home position.
Three distinct gaits are implemented: a crawl gait for statically stable locomotion, where legs move
sequentially in a diagonal pattern; a trot gait for faster movement, where diagonal leg pairs move
simultaneously; and a turning gait, where left and right legs move in opposite directions to
generate rotational torque. Servo movements are interpolated over time with configurable speed,
ensuring smooth motion and preventing sudden movements that could upset the robot's balance. All
movements are defined relative to a home position, which serves as the reference point for
calibration and initialization. Since the lookup table was measured for the left legs, the right
legs use mirrored values to maintain symmetry in movement.

The software architecture is organized into four functional layers. The lowest layer abstracts the
hardware interface, managing communication with the PWM driver and providing a unified interface for
setting servo angles. Above this sits the kinematics layer, which implements the lookup table and
interpolation logic to translate desired foot positions into joint angles. The gait generation layer
defines the periodic leg movement patterns, swing and stance trajectories, and orchestrates
their execution across multiple legs according to the selected gait pattern. As discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:literature-review}, statically stable gaits require that the center of gravity
remains within the support polygon formed by the grounded feet; the crawl gait satisfies this
criterion by moving only one leg at a time, while the trot gait achieves faster locomotion by moving
diagonal pairs simultaneously, trading some stability margin for speed. The highest layer comprises
the application interfaces that allow human operators to command the robot. The primary control
interface is a web server running on the Raspberry Pi that exposes an HTTP-based remote control
endpoint. HTTP is a request-response application protocol operating over TCP, in which the client
(the operator's browser) initiates a connection and sends a request, and the server returns a
response. The server listens on a dedicated port and handles two types of requests. A GET request
to the root path returns the HTML control page, which contains a JavaScript component that captures
keyboard events. When the operator presses a key, the script issues an asynchronous POST request
carrying the movement command to a specific endpoint path. The server parses the request path to
determine the desired action and, because HTTP itself is stateless, maintains an internal state
machine to track whether the robot is idle, standing, or executing a gait. Upon receiving a movement
command, the server transitions to the moving state and spawns a background execution thread so that
the gait can run without blocking the communication interface. A timeout mechanism automatically
returns the robot to a resting position if no further commands are received within a few seconds,
preventing the robot from remaining in an active stance indefinitely. This design prioritizes
responsiveness and simplicity, making the robot accessible to operators without requiring
specialized control hardware or software installation. For development and debugging, separate
command-line utilities allow individual servos to be positioned manually. These tools were essential
during the calibration phase and for verifying mechanical assembly, as it enables direct control of
any joint without invoking the full gait system, as well as settings servos to desired angles for
collecting lookup measurements.

\subsection{Object Detection Model}
\label{subsec:object-detection-models}
Before discussing the YOLO architecture, it is important to understand the underlying technology
that makes it possible: the Convolutional Neural Network (CNN). A CNN is a class of deep neural
networks specifically designed for processing grid-structured data such as images. Unlike
traditional fully connected networks, where every neuron is connected to every neuron in the
adjacent layer, a CNN employs convolutional layers that apply learnable filters (kernels) across
local regions of the input. Each filter detects specific visual features of a given image,
such as edges, textures, shapes, or increasingly abstract patterns, by computing dot products
between the filter weights and the local pixel values. The output of this operation is a feature map
that preserves the spatial relationships within the image. CNNs also incorporate pooling layers,
which progressively reduce the spatial dimensions of the feature maps, thereby decreasing
computational complexity and providing a degree of translation invariance. This hierarchical feature
extraction, combined with weight sharing across spatial locations, makes CNNs exceptionally
effective for computer vision tasks while requiring far fewer parameters than their fully connected
counterparts.

YOLO introduces the most innovative approach to object recognition. Before, systems like Deformable
Parts Model (DPM) and Regression-based Convolutional Neural Network (R-CNN) repurposed classifiers
to perform detection. Classifiers for each object were used to evaluate it at various locations and
scales in a test image in a sliding windows manner or on potential bounding boxes. Then the
post-processing stage would refine the bounding boxes and remove duplicates. Such cumbersome
pipelines were slow and difficult to optimize because of heterogeneous nature of these components.
The new approach that YOLO introduces uses the whole images to train the model to predict objects,
which converts object detection to a single regression problem. This method uses a single
convolution network to predict multiple bounding boxes and class probabilities for these boxes
simultaneously, which make it swift by simplifying the pipeline. Unlike the aforementioned
mechanisms, YOLO can reason globally by examining the whole image, which gives it an advantage of
learning generalized representations of objects.

The model starts by dividing the input image into $S \times S$ grid. Each grid cell is responsible
for detecting bounding boxes, their confidence, and class probabilities. Grid cells are not limited
to one bounding box or class, instead, they can have $B$ boxes but only one set of $C$ classes
probabilities per cell. In the original paper the authors suggest 2 and 20 respectively on a
$7 \times 7$ grid. Each of these bounding boxes is made up of 5 numbers: the x and y positions,
representing the location of the center of the predicted bounding box; the width and height relative
to the entire image size; and the confidence score, representing the predicted Intersection over
Union (IoU) between the bounding box and the ground truth. Formally,
\begin{equation}
\text{confidence} = \Pr(\text{object}) \times \text{IoU}_{\text{pred}}^{\text{truth}}.
\end{equation}
If no object exists in the cell, the confidence score should ideally be zero. The class
probabilities are conditioned on the cell containing an object, denoted
$\Pr(\text{class}_i \mid \text{object})$. At test time, the class-specific confidence for each box
is obtained by multiplying the conditional class probabilities by the box confidence:
\begin{equation}
\Pr(\text{class}_i \mid \text{object}) \times \Pr(\text{object}) \times
\text{IoU}_{\text{pred}}^{\text{truth}} =
\Pr(\text{class}_i) \times \text{IoU}_{\text{pred}}^{\text{truth}}.
\end{equation}
This product encodes both the probability that the box contains an object of class $i$ and how well
the box fits the object.

The network architecture borrows heavily from GoogLeNet, also known as Inception-v1. It is made up
of 24 convolutional layers followed by 2 fully connected layers at the end. The convolutional layers
handle feature extraction, starting with simple edges and textures and building up to more complex
patterns, while the fully connected layers map these features to the final output tensor of size
$S \times S \times (B \times 5 + C)$.

For training, the authors chose Sum-Squared Error (SSE) as the loss function. This single loss
covers three things at once: the accuracy of bounding box coordinates, the confidence score, and
the class prediction. A challenge here was that most grid cells in any image do not contain an
object, which can bias the model toward always predicting ``no object.'' To prevent this, the loss
function applies different weights to different parts of the prediction. Coordinate errors are
multiplied by $\lambda_{\text{coord}} = 5$ to increase their importance, while confidence errors
for empty boxes are scaled down by $\lambda_{\text{noobj}} = 0.5$. This ensures that the network
pays more attention to getting the boxes right when objects are actually present.

Because every cell predicts multiple bounding boxes, the network naturally generates many
overlapping detections around the same object. To deal with this, YOLO runs non-maximum suppression
(NMS) as a post-processing step. NMS works by selecting the box with the highest confidence and
removing any other box that overlaps with it significantly, meaning any box with a high IoU.
The end result is a single, high-confidence bounding box per object rather than multiple
overlapping predictions.

Since its original release, YOLO has evolved through several major architectural shifts. Early
iterations moved from pure regression to anchor-based detection, introduced batch normalization
and multi-scale training, and adopted deeper residual backbones with feature pyramid-style
predictions at multiple resolutions. The next wave of improvements focused on training efficiency
and deployment accessibility: Cross-Stage Partial networks reduced computational cost, the
ecosystem migrated from Darknet to native PyTorch, and automated augmentation and anchor
calculation removed much of the manual tuning. More recently, the architecture has shifted toward
anchor-free detection, decoupled classification and regression heads, and unified frameworks that
integrate object detection with segmentation and pose estimation under a single model family. For
this project, I decided to use the Ultralytics YOLO library with the YOLOv8 model, which implements
a modern version of the architecture with these advancements built in. It offers a good trade-off
between real-time performance and detection accuracy, which is critical when running on a
resource-constrained platform like the Raspberry Pi. This setup allows the robot to reliably detect
objects at frame rates that are sufficient for real-world interaction.

As mentioned before in the requirements of the project, the selected objects to train the model on
are dogs and humans. I chose the most significant datasets in the history of object detection: COCO
and Stanford Dogs. COCO contains over $330,000$ images and more than $2.5$ million labeled instances
across 91 object categories, while the latter contains over $22,000$ annotated images of dogs
belonging to 120 species. Even though the datasets are great as they are, they should be adapted to
the case I have, which is a close-to-ground quadruped robot. The average height of the camera of
the robot in the standing position is $17$~cm with an upward tilt of $15^\circ$.

To understand why adaptation is necessary, it helps to look at what the camera actually sees. The
camera has a horizontal field of view of $54^\circ$ and a vertical field of view of $41^\circ$. The
optical axis is tilted $15^\circ$ above horizontal, so the vertical field of view is split around
this axis: the top edge of the frame is $15^\circ + 20.5^\circ = 35.5^\circ$ above horizontal, and
the bottom edge is $15^\circ - 20.5^\circ = 5.5^\circ$ below horizontal.

At a horizontal distance $d$ from the robot, the height of the top edge of the frame is
\begin{equation}
y_{\text{top}} = 17 + d \cdot \tan(35.5^\circ) \approx 17 + 0.713d \text{ cm},
\end{equation}
and the height of the bottom edge is
\begin{equation}
y_{\text{bottom}} = 17 - d \cdot \tan(5.5^\circ) \approx 17 - 0.096d \text{ cm}.
\end{equation}
The factor $0.713$ comes from $\tan(35.5^\circ)$ and the factor $0.096$ comes from
$\tan(5.5^\circ)$. The total vertical span of the frame at distance $d$ is the difference between
these two values, which works out to approximately $0.809d$~cm. Anything outside the band
$[y_{\text{bottom}}, y_{\text{top}}]$ is cut off.

The horizontal width of the view is determined by the $54^\circ$ horizontal field of view. With half
of that angle being $27^\circ$, the total width is
\begin{equation}
w = 2d \cdot \tan(27^\circ) \approx 1.019d \text{ cm}.
\end{equation}

The numbers make the problem clear. At the distances where the robot is likely to encounter humans
and dogs, roughly $50$--$150$~cm, the camera captures the lower portion of humans and most of
a dog's body. At $50$~cm, only about a quarter of a human is visible, mostly lower legs and feet.
Dogs fare much better because of their smaller size, with three-quarters or more of the body
typically in frame. As the distance increases, more of a human comes into view, but the perspective
remains unusual: the camera looks up from below rather than straight on. This is a very different
viewpoint from the standard dataset images, which are usually taken at eye level and show complete
objects. Training on those images without adjustment would leave the model unprepared for the
low-angle, from-below perspective that the robot actually experiences.

To address the issue, I create a balanced dataset by combining the original COCO and Stanford Dogs
datasets that undergo data transformations to simulate the robot's perspective as well as to mimic
variations in camera exposure, color calibration, and image quality. The balanced dataset contains
cropped images of people and dogs as well as their bounding box annotations. Additionally to that,
the dataset contains $10\%$ of negative sample images, which are images that do not contain any of
the target objects. After preparing the dataset, I split it into training and validation sets, with
an $80$--$20$ split, which results into $12,500$ training images, where $5,000$ contain people,
$5,000$ contain dogs, and $2,500$ are negative samples. The validation set contains $2,500$ images:
$1,000$ with people, $1,000$ with dogs, and $500$ negative samples.

The model is trained on a MacBook Pro with an Apple M2 Pro chip with 16 core GPU. The training
utilzes the Metal Performance Shaders (MPS) backend, which is Apple's framework, designed on top of
Metal graphics API, for accelerating machine learning workloads on GPU. Architecturally, MPS
abstracts the complexity of writing low-level Metal Shading Language (MSL) by providing a suite of
pre-compiled, mathematically optimized compute kernels, which are small parallelized mathematical
operations, such as multi-dimensional matrix multiplications and convolutional operations. The
execution of these kernels is orchestrated by PyTorch's dynamic computational graph, which maps out
the sequence of mathematical operations and data dependencies required by the model. When this graph
is dispatched to the ``mps'' device backend, the framework analyzes the graph's structure to select
and schedule the precise kernels needed for execution. This process leverages Apple Silicon’s
Unified Memory Architecture (UMA), executing a zero-copy memory allocation scheme where the CPU and
GPU access a singular, shared pool of physical RAM without the latency-inducing overhead of PCIe bus
transfers. Consequently, MPS compiles these graph operations into specialized Pipeline State Objects
(PSOs) and commits them to a Metal Command Buffer, which the hardware schedules across highly
parallelized GPU compute cores. This tight integration Apple's hardware in PyTorch allows to use
the full potential of the hardware, which results in significantly faster training times compared to
CPU-only execution.

An additional factor that contributes to the speed of training is the correct use of training
parameters. After many experiments, I found that the best resulting parameters in terms of accuracy
and training time are: batch size of $8$, epochs of $50$, and $0$ workers. Additionally, the image
size parameter is set to $320$ so the model can run in real-time on the Raspberry Pi. The final
command for training is:
\begin{minted}{bash}
PYTORCH_MPS_HIGH_WATERMARK_RATIO=0.0 \
yolo detect train model=yolov8n.pt \
data=data.yaml \
imgsz=320 \
batch=8 \
epochs=50 \
device=mps \
workers=0
\end{minted}
where:
\begin{description}
  \item[\texttt{PYTORCH\_MPS\_HIGH\_WATERMARK\_RATIO=0.0}] An environment variable for PyTorch's MPS
backend; setting it to \texttt{0.0} allows the MPS allocator to use memory more aggressively to
avoid OOM errors on Apple Silicon.
  \item[\texttt{yolo detect train}] The Ultralytics CLI command that selects the YOLO detection task
and starts a training run.
  \item[\texttt{model=yolov8n.pt}] The initial weights to load (YOLOv8 Nano). This uses a small,
fast pretrained model as a transfer-learning starting point.
  \item[\texttt{data=data.yaml}] Path to the dataset configuration YAML describing
training/validation image locations and class names.
  \item[\texttt{imgsz=320}] Input image size (images are resized to 320×320). Smaller sizes reduce
memory and speed up training at the cost of some small-object accuracy, acceptable for real-time
applications.
  \item[\texttt{batch=8}] Number of images processed per optimization step. Lower batch sizes reduce
memory usage and increases training speed.
  \item[\texttt{epochs=50}] Number of full passes over the training set.
  \item[\texttt{device=mps}] Use the Apple Metal Performance Shaders (MPS) GPU backend on macOS
instead of the CPU.
  \item[\texttt{workers=0}] Disable background data loader subprocesses (safer on macOS to avoid
multiprocessing issues).
\end{description}
This configuration is well-optimized for training on consumer hardware while still achieving good
accuracy. The training process typically take around $2.5$ hours to complete on this hardware. The
recorded training curves show steady convergence: all training losses (bounding-box, class,
and distribution focal loss) decrease consistently, which indicates the model learned from the
data. A final validation pass (performed after training because validation was disabled during
the loop) produced very high aggregate metrics: precision $\approx 96.5\%$, recall $\approx 94.5\%$,
mAP@50 $\approx 98.7\%$, and mAP@50:95 $\approx 97.8\%$. At the same time the validation
classification loss is noticeably larger than the training classification loss (val~$\approx 0.70$
vs. train~$\approx 0.09$), suggesting some degree of overfitting on class predictions. These results
are encouraging for a lightweight `yolov8n` model running at $320\times320$.
