\chapter{Introduction}
Over the past four decades, there has been a major leap in robotics, especially in the field of
quadruped robots. Many important papers developing the field have been published, leading to
significant advancements. The development progressed from early one-legged hopping machines to
four-legged trotting robots able to traverse difficult terrain and obstacles, and later to
autonomous navigation in unstructured environments. Legged locomotion has shown itself to be one
of the most versatile forms of mobility. However, most of this research was conducted in
laboratories, requiring substantial resources for prototyping and pushing the boundaries of the
field. This creates a gap between laboratory research and consumer-level accessibility.

The objective of this thesis is to address that gap by designing, constructing, and controlling
a quadruped robot as an integrated project. The work covers the full development pipeline:
mechanical design using modern CAD tools and 3D printing, selection and characterization of
actuators, implementation of statically stable gaits based on established stability criteria,
development of a control system, integration of computer vision, and preparation for physical
validation. By following this end-to-end process, the thesis aims to demonstrate how classical
locomotion and computer vision principles can be realized on a budget-friendly platform.

A deliberate choice was made to use low-cost, commercially available components and
rapid-prototyping techniques wherever possible. This decision was not driven by cost reduction
alone; it also helps ensure that the resulting platform is reproducible. In this paper, I review
research and practical achievements made throughout the years and combine them in one project at a
lower cost. The idea is to establish a base for low-cost quadruped robots and make them more
affordable for day-to-day use in different fields of everyday life.

\section{Literature review}
\label{sec:literature-review}
There are several ``classical'' papers that can be used as a basis for this project. They cover
topics ranging from gaits and control to computer vision and successful real-world systems.

The most important capability of a quadruped robot is locomotion. The mathematical analysis in the
1968 \textit{Mathematical Biosciences} article ``On the stability properties of quadruped creeping
gaits'' shows that there is ``a unique optimum gait that maximizes static
stability'' \parencite[331]{mcghee1968}. The work explains that static stability requires the
projection of the center of gravity onto the ground to remain within the support polygon. This
polygon is formed by connecting all feet that are in contact with the ground, where each foot
represents one contact point.

However, stability can also be achieved by dynamic means. The concept of dynamic stability is
discussed in the 1984 article ``Experiments in Balance with a 3D One-Legged Hopping Machine'',
which describes control algorithms for forward velocity, body attitude, and hopping
height~\parencite{raibert1984}. Unlike static locomotion, dynamic locomotion allows the center of
gravity to move outside the support region for short periods. Balance is then maintained through
continuous motion and active control rather than through a permanently stable body position. Moving
the projection of the center of gravity outside the support region causes the robot to start
falling. During this falling motion, the robot places its feet to catch itself, and repeating this
sequence creates a dynamic gait. In other words, dynamic stability is continuous falling and
catching.

In recent years, computing power has become more accessible, while the development of neural
networks has enabled the creation of increasingly realistic simulated environments. Digital
versions of robots can be placed in these environments and given a task, such as moving from point
A to point B, without receiving an explicitly programmed sequence of joint movements. Instead, the
robot learns a movement policy by interacting with the simulated environment through Deep
Reinforcement Learning (DRL). This approach was used for the ANYmal robot in the 2019 article
``Learning Agile and Dynamic Motor Skills for Legged Robots''~\parencite{hwangbo2019}. The
researchers incorporated the real-world behavior of the robot's motors into the simulation. This
made the simulation more realistic and helped the learned policy account for motor imperfections and
other differences between the simulated and physical systems.

While the theoretical foundations establish how quadruped locomotion works, several landmark systems
demonstrate what is achievable with mature engineering. Boston Dynamics' BigDog demonstrated
load-carrying dynamic locomotion on rough terrain and was one of the first systems to show that
quadrupeds could operate in real-world conditions outside the laboratory~\parencite{bigdog}. It
directly continued ideas from the 1984 hopping-machine experiments and made it evident that dynamic
stability is the key to traversing challenging terrain. Similarly, MIT's Cheetah 3 showed that
model-based control could achieve robust blind locomotion over obstacles~\parencite{cheetah3}.
These systems represented state-of-the-art quadruped capabilities at the time. The projects were
sponsored by DARPA and MIT, respectively, and did not focus on cost reduction, but rather on pushing
the boundaries of what quadruped robots could do.

Another key component of the project is computer vision. Generally, robots can be controlled by a
human operator, which remains a very practical approach. However, computer vision is a significant
step towards autonomy and high-level task execution. Vision is critical for navigation, obstacle
avoidance, and interaction with the environment. For this project, I decided to use YOLO,
a real-time object detection model based on the You Only Look Once
architecture~\parencite{yolo}. The original model divides an image into a grid, and each grid cell
is responsible for predicting bounding boxes, confidence scores, and class probabilities for an
object whose center falls into that cell. As described in the paper:
``[The] network architecture is inspired by the GoogLeNet model for image classification\ldots\ [It]
has 24 convolutional layers followed by 2 fully connected layers. Instead of the inception modules
used by GoogLeNet,\ldots [it simply uses] $1 \times 1$ reduction layers followed by $3 \times 3$
convolutional layers\ldots''~\parencite[780]{yolo}. The original YOLO uses a Sum-Squared Error (SSE)
loss function between predictions and ground truth. While the architecture has evolved
significantly, some later YOLO versions adopted CSPDarknet, a high-performance convolutional neural
network backbone, together with more advanced loss functions. Other versions use different
backbones, feature-aggregation methods, and detection heads. Nevertheless, single-pass detection
remains a central principle.

Additionally, it is worth mentioning ``Object Detection with Discriminatively Trained Part-Based
Models''~\parencite{felzenszwalb2010}. This classical paper describes how objects can be detected
from their individual parts. The concept is relevant to this project because the robot's low camera
position may allow it to see only the lower part of a person or another object. Training the model
to recognize partially visible objects helps the robot detect objects from its unusual viewpoint.

While the model architecture and the approach to training it are important, the datasets used for
training enable the model to reach its full potential. In this project, I utilize two of the most
significant benchmarks in the history of object detection: Common Objects in Context
(COCO)~\parencite{coco} and Stanford Dogs~\parencite{stanforddogs}. COCO established the standard
for detecting objects in ``natural'' settings rather than perfectly centered studio shots. It
contains more than $330,000$ images, of which over $200,000$ are labelled, with approximately $1.5$
million object instances across 80 object categories, including people, dogs, and cats. The Stanford
Dogs dataset contains $20,580$ annotated images of dogs belonging to 120 breeds. Each image is
annotated with a bounding box and a class label.

\chapter{Methodology}
\section{Methods and technologies used}
Developing a functional quadruped robot on a limited budget requires navigating a wide landscape
of hardware components, software frameworks, and design methodologies. While the robotics field
offers a rich variety of actuators, computing platforms, sensors, and control strategies, the
project constraints significantly narrow the range of viable options. Cost, reproducibility, and
the mechanical demands of legged locomotion impose trade-offs at every stage of the design
process, forcing a careful balance between performance, affordability, and ease of integration.
The following subsections outline the requirements that shaped this design space and present the
technologies and models selected to address them.

\subsection{Project requirements}
\label{subsec:project-requirements}
There are several requirements and limitations in this project, as it seeks to demonstrate a way
to develop a low-cost quadruped robot. Three main groups of limitations define the requirements.
The first, and most significant, is cost. It influences the second group, which is hardware. The
hardware, limited by cost, in turn constrains the software and algorithms. Together, they create a
tight framework for the project, which is beneficial because it encourages creativity and finding
new solutions.

My requirements for the project are the following:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The robot should be able to walk on flat surfaces in all directions: forward, backward,
  sideways, and turning in place. Flat surfaces were selected because rough terrain is too
  challenging for the hardware used in this project.
    \item The robot should have 12 degrees of freedom (DOF), where each joint that can move
independently contributes one DOF, to allow for a variety of gaits and maneuvers. Each leg
should have 3 DOF: hip abduction and adduction (side-to-side movement), hip flexion and extension
(forward-backward movement), and knee flexion and extension.
    \item It should have computer vision capabilities to detect objects. For the sake of
demonstration, I chose people and dogs as the target classes because the robot is expected to
encounter and interact with them.
    \item The robot should be easy to assemble and reproduce by others. The components should be
  available, affordable, and designed with swappability in mind to allow future upgrades and
  repairs.
\end{itemize}

On the limitation side, the total cost of the project should not exceed 300 EUR\@. This
significantly limits what the project can achieve.

The hardware and software choices are affected by this limitation, and the technology selection is
discussed in the next section.
\subsection{Technology selection}
\label{subsec:technology-selection}
Everything starts with the model. Nowadays, 3D printers are quite accessible, making them a great
choice for this project because they allow custom parts to be produced according to the design
requirements while remaining reproducible by others. At my disposal were the Bambu Lab A1 mini,
X1 Carbon, and H2D printers. I tried them all and settled on the H2D because it has two nozzles,
allowing PLA and PETG to be used in the same print. PLA is a suitable general-purpose material for
the robot itself, while PETG is used for support interfaces. It is possible to use only PLA, and I
tested that approach, but PETG makes support removal easier because it does not bond strongly to
PLA\@. I designed the robot in Onshape, a free cloud-based CAD web application with sufficient
functionality for this project. For the slicer, I used Bambu Studio, which works well with the
printers and provides the required functionality. A slicer converts 3D models into G-code,
specifying toolpaths, layer heights, support placement, and other print parameters.

I did not create the initial model myself, as doing so would require much more time. Instead, I
used a suitable model by Tom Knox, published under a Creative Commons 4.0 license, and modified it
to meet the requirements~\parencite{knox}. The requirements not met by the initial model were 12 DOF
and ease of assembly. What the original model did provide was the base design I wanted, three DOF
in each front leg, and a place for the camera module.

What made the original 3D model hard to assemble was the fact that it consisted of 149 unique parts
connected by different types of screws. The most problematic part was the leg. All four legs are
the same, just mirrored, which made redesigning much easier. To reduce assembly complexity, I
redesigned the legs and body to be print-in-place, meaning ready-to-use parts without the need for
additional assembly. I also reduced the number of unique screws in the project to one type, M3, in
two sizes.

For the actuators, I chose WS-MG996R digital servos with metal gears, which are widely used in
hobby robotics. Three servos per leg provide 12 DOF in total. These servos are affordable and
provide a specified stall torque of \(9.4\,\mathrm{kgf}\mathbin{\cdot}\mathrm{cm}\) at 4.8 V and
\(11\,\mathrm{kgf}\mathbin{\cdot}\mathrm{cm}\) at 6 V. The latter is approximately
\(1.08\,\mathrm{N}\mathbin{\cdot}\mathrm{m}\). Their specified speed is $0.17$ seconds per
$60^\circ$ at 4.8 V and $0.14$ seconds per $60^\circ$ at 6 V, and their specified operating
voltage range is 4.8--7.2 V~\parencite{mg996r}. However, these low-cost actuators have limited
precision and torque, and they do not expose position feedback to the controller. Considering these
limitations, they are the minimum suitable option for this project.

For the main controller, I chose a Raspberry Pi 4 Model B with 4 GB of RAM\@. It is
powerful enough to control the servos and run a small object detection model. It uses a 64-bit
Broadcom BCM2711
quad-core Cortex-A72 processor running at up to 1.8 GHz and a VideoCore VI GPU with support for
OpenGL ES 3.1 and Vulkan 1.0. It also provides dual-band Wi-Fi, Bluetooth 5.0 BLE, Gigabit Ethernet,
two USB 3.0 ports, two USB 2.0 ports, a 40-pin GPIO header, a two-lane MIPI CSI camera port, a
two-lane MIPI DSI display port, USB-C power input, and 4 GB LPDDR4-3200 SDRAM\@. A big advantage of
this computer is that it runs Raspberry Pi OS, a Linux-based operating system that allows fast
prototyping and the use of existing programs and development tools~\parencite{raspberrypi4}.

The Raspberry Pi provides enough general-purpose pins for 12 control signals, but only a small
number support hardware PWM\@. Software-generated PWM under Linux can also have timing jitter. I
therefore used a PCA9685, a 16-channel, 12-bit PWM driver that generates stable servo signals in
hardware. It communicates over I2C using two signal lines: SDA on GPIO 2 and SCL on GPIO 3. It is
also connected to 3.3 V for logic power and to a common ground~\parencite{pca9685}.

The specified stall current is 2.5 A per servo at 6 V~\parencite{mg996r}. Using this value, a
theoretical simultaneous stall of all 12 servos would require 30 A, although this is not the normal
operating current. To power the robot, I decided to use a battery pack made from four 3500 mAh 18650
lithium-ion batteries~\parencite{lgmj1}. Connected in a 2S2P configuration (two series pairs in
parallel), they provide a nominal voltage of 7.2 V and a total nominal capacity of 7000 mAh. Since
the Raspberry Pi requires a 5 V supply, the buck converters will provide 7 V for the servos and 5 V
for the Raspberry Pi.

For computer vision, I use a 5-megapixel Raspberry Pi Camera Module v1.3, which features
an OmniVision OV5647 color CMOS sensor. The camera has a 3.6 mm focal length lens with a
$54^\circ \times 41^\circ$
angle of view, providing a horizontal field of view roughly equal to the distance from the subject
at typical operating ranges~\parencite{cameraV1}. The camera uses a CSI ribbon cable to connect to
the dedicated MIPI CSI port on the Raspberry Pi, offering lower communication overhead than a
typical USB camera.

On the software side, I use Python as the main programming language. It is a great choice for rapid
prototyping and has a large ecosystem of libraries for robotics, computer vision, and machine
learning. For controlling the servos, I use the Adafruit PCA9685 library, which provides a simple
interface for generating PWM control signals. PWM control does not regulate the servo's supply
voltage. Instead, the pulse width encodes the requested position. Typical control pulses range from
approximately 1 to 2 milliseconds and are repeated at a fixed rate. The exact pulse-to-angle range
depends on servo calibration~\parencite{adafruitpca9685,mg996r}. For object detection, I use the
Ultralytics YOLO library, which offers a streamlined interface for YOLO training and inference and
integrates well with the Raspberry Pi platform~\parencite{ultralytics}.
\subsection{Implementation}
\label{subsec:implementation}
The implementation started by downloading the original model and printing its most important part:
the leg. The original leg model consisted of seven parts and took around three hours to print.
Afterward, I assembled the leg, attached the hip to a servo, and connected the servo to the
Raspberry Pi with jumper wires for an initial test. Printing and testing the leg were crucial steps
that helped me verify that the model could move. Having the physical model also helped me understand
what needed to be changed.

Later, I started redesigning the leg to be print-in-place. First of all, it is important to
understand the principles of such a design. The most important idea is that unsupported overhangs
should be minimized, meaning that the parts should be supported by the print itself where possible.
To achieve this, I used $45^\circ$ as a practical overhang limit, beyond which support material was
generally required. Another thing to keep in mind is the clearance between the moving parts.
Through experimentation, I found that 0.65 mm provides the best balance between smooth motion and
minimal play, so this value was used for most clearances in the model. The layer height for every
print was 0.2 mm, and support material was enabled for overhanging parts such as the curves of the
legs and the body ``ribs''.

The final leg consists of six elements: motor extension, extension link, shoulder disk, femur,
secondary link, and tibia, printed as one module. After removing it from the printer bed and
detaching the supports, it is movable and ready to be installed. To get to the final design, I went
through around twenty iterations of the leg and a couple of iterations of the body. Several design
challenges emerged during these iterations, and to achieve the requirements while using the same
design, I had to make exceptions to the basic principles. For example, a support blocker is used to
prevent support from being generated inside the motor extension, creating a bridging surface that
spans a gap without support.

Another example showing the challenge of designing print-in-place models is the knee. The knee
joint employs a cylindrical pivot pin that is integrated into the tibia (lower leg), with the femur
(upper leg) captured between the two halves of the tibia joint, forming a sandwich-like structure.
This arrangement creates overhangs on both the top and bottom surfaces of the femur where it
interfaces with the tibia. Support material is therefore required in two places: between the upper
tibia surface and the femur, and between the femur and the lower tibia surface. The overhanging
surface is large and could droop and fuse to the other part, after which it could not be recovered.
Support was therefore necessary, so unfortunately, each knee contains a small amount of PETG
support material. PLA can also be used as an alternative support material, but it must be broken
free by moving the knee joint after printing.

Beyond the main structural parts, the model contains eight servo holders that are printed
separately. Each holder weighs 5 grams and does not require support material. The holders attach to
the servos with screws, while the connection to the body uses integrated pins that slot into
corresponding holes, eliminating the need for additional fasteners. In total, printing the complete
model requires approximately 20.5 hours and 490 grams of filament. The body takes 7 hours and 154
grams, each of the four legs requires 3 hours and 74 grams, and the eight servo holders together
take 1.5 hours and 40 grams.

When the whole model was stable and ready, I started assembling it all together. With the
experience I gained, I estimate that it would take around one hour to assemble everything.
Connecting the PCA9685 to the Raspberry Pi was a straightforward process. The control-pin
connection layout is the following: VCC to 3.3 V, GND to the common ground, SDA to GPIO 2, and SCL
to GPIO 3. The PCA9685 logic supply does not power the servos; instead, the board has separate V+
and GND terminals for the servo power supply. These are connected to an external power supply.
While testing, I used a laboratory power supply set to 6 V with a 6 A current limit, which was
enough for testing.

In order to enable the I2C communication interface on the Raspberry Pi, I enabled it through
\texttt{sudo raspi-config} under Interface Options, I2C. After that, I installed the I2C utilities
by running \texttt{sudo} \texttt{apt} \texttt{install} \texttt{i2c-tools}. The connection can be
verified by running \texttt{i2cdetect} \texttt{-y} \texttt{1}, which should show the PCA9685 at
its default 7-bit address,
\texttt{0x40}. In the I2C protocol, each device requires a unique 7-bit address so that the
controller can identify it. The PCA9685 uses a partially configurable address scheme: the most
significant bit is fixed, while the lower six bits can be modified using the external address pins
$A_0$ through $A_5$. With all six pins low, the binary address is $1000000$, corresponding to
\texttt{0x40}. This design allows up to 62 PCA9685 boards to coexist on the same bus. For example,
setting $A_0$ high while keeping the other pins low changes the address to \texttt{0x41}. For this
project, I use the default address, \texttt{0x40}~\parencite{pca9685}.

The other pins form 16 channels for PWM output, which are connected to the servos. Each channel
consists of three pins: V+ for power, GND, and OUT for the control signal. I grouped them by leg in
the following order: hip extension, knee flexion, and hip abduction, so it is easier to understand
which channel controls each joint. The PCA9685 board provides sockets for the servos, so they can be
connected without soldering. However, the board exposes the I2C pins, while the Raspberry Pi exposes
its GPIO header, so I soldered wires to connect them.

Each servo has a $180^\circ$ range of motion, so before attaching the servos to the robot, I set the
hip extension to $90^\circ$, knee flexion to $60^\circ$, and hip abduction to $95^\circ$ as their
home positions. These values are defined in the code, and all movements are relative to them. Then,
the servos are attached as follows. The hip-abduction servo is fixed vertically in its slot in the
body, and a dual-arm horn is attached to its output shaft. After that, the hip-extension and
knee-flexion servos are placed together and secured with servo holders on the sides. Another
dual-arm horn is attached to the knee-flexion servo, while a round disc horn is fixed to the rear
side of the femur. Finally, the horns are connected to the hip and motor extension, respectively.
The same procedure is repeated for all four legs. After that, the electronics are installed inside
the body, and the Raspberry Pi camera module is mounted in the front camera slot. With the
mechanical assembly complete, the control software can command movement.

A step consists of two phases: swing and stance. During the swing phase, the foot leaves the ground
and moves to the next contact position. During the stance phase, the foot remains in contact with
the ground and supports the robot. For forward movement, it moves backward relative to the chassis,
generating the ground reaction force that pushes the robot's body forward. A gait defines how the
steps of all four legs are timed and coordinated.

In this implementation, the swing and stance phases are represented using trajectories. During the
swing trajectory, the foot moves linearly in the horizontal direction while its vertical position
follows a sine-shaped arch:
\begin{equation}
x(t) = x_{\text{start}} + (x_{\text{end}} - x_{\text{start}})t, \qquad
y(t) = y_{\text{ground}} + L\sin(\pi t),
\end{equation}
where $t$ ranges from 0 to 1 and $L$ is the lift height. This arch is smooth and resembles a
parabola, but the implementation uses a sine function rather than a mathematical parabola. For the
same lift height and step duration, the sine profile has a lower vertical speed at lift-off and
touchdown than a parabolic profile, making these transitions less abrupt. During the stance
trajectory, the horizontal position changes linearly in the opposite direction while $y$ remains
at $y_{\text{ground}}$.

To translate desired foot positions into servo angles, the system uses a lookup-table approach
rather than analytical inverse kinematics. This method was chosen because the mechanical coupling
in the leg design, where hip movement affects the effective knee geometry, makes a simple
analytical model impractical. The lookup table contains pre-calibrated mappings
between joint angles and foot positions, created by setting different servo angles and measuring
the resulting foot positions. With the hip mounting point defined as the coordinate origin, the
foot position is expressed in millimeters relative to this reference. The calibration data
consists of 81 measurements spanning the feasible range of hip and knee angles. Each entry records
the hip angle,
knee angle, and the corresponding foot coordinates, capturing the reachable workspace of the leg.
For example, hip and knee angles of $40^\circ$ and $65^\circ$ correspond to the foot position
$(6, -160)$~mm. The neighboring angle pairs $(40^\circ, 70^\circ)$ and
$(40^\circ, 75^\circ)$ correspond to $(-3, -165)$~mm and $(-12, -168)$~mm, respectively.
When the control system requests a target foot position $(x_t, y_t)$, it locates the nearest
calibration points in the two-dimensional position space and interpolates between them using inverse
distance weighting~\parencite{shepard1968}. For each calibration point $i$ with foot position
$(x_i, y_i)$, the Euclidean distance to the target is computed as
\begin{equation}
d_i = \sqrt{(x_t - x_i)^2 + (y_t - y_i)^2}.
\end{equation}
The four nearest points are selected, and a weight is assigned to each according to the inverse
square of its distance:
\begin{equation}
w_i = \frac{1}{d_i^{\,2}}.
\end{equation}
These weights are normalized so that they sum to unity,
\begin{equation}
\hat{w}_i = \frac{w_i}{\sum_{j=1}^{4} w_j},
\end{equation}
and the interpolated joint angles are obtained as the weighted average of the corresponding
calibration angles:
\begin{equation}
\theta_{\text{hip}} = \sum_{i=1}^{4} \hat{w}_i \, \theta_{\text{hip},i}, \qquad
\theta_{\text{knee}} = \sum_{i=1}^{4} \hat{w}_i \, \theta_{\text{knee},i}.
\end{equation}

In practice, the implementation includes several practical adjustments: if the target lies within
$0.1$~mm of a calibration point, the stored angles are returned directly without interpolation;
distances below $0.001$~mm are clamped to prevent division by zero; and the final interpolated
angles are rounded to the nearest integer because the current software represents commands in
whole degrees. This is a software choice, not a limitation of the PCA9685 or servo. The method
assigns greater influence to closer calibration points, producing interpolated angle estimates for
positions that were not explicitly measured. The approach is particularly suitable for this
mechanism because the mechanical coupling and manufacturing tolerances make an accurate closed-form
analytical model difficult to derive. While inverse kinematics is the conventional approach in
robotics literature, empirical mapping provides a practical alternative when mechanical complexity
or manufacturing tolerances make analytical models unreliable. The
abduction joints, which control side-to-side leg movement, are not included in the
lookup table because their motion is kinematically decoupled from the primary leg swing mechanism.
These joints operate independently and are controlled through direct angle commands. Each servo is
individually calibrated to account for mechanical variations and assembly differences between left
and right legs. A direction multiplier ensures that positive commands produce consistent physical
movements across all legs, while center offsets compensate for variations in the home position.
Three hip-and-knee gait functions are implemented. In the crawl function, the legs swing in the
order left front, right rear, right front, and left rear. Because the function moves one leg at a
time, three feet remain on the ground, although the actual static stability still depends on the
center-of-gravity position as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:literature-review}. In the trot gait,
the left-front and right-rear legs form one diagonal pair, while the right-front and left-rear legs
form the other. One pair follows the swing trajectory while the other follows the stance trajectory,
and the pairs then exchange roles. In the turning gait, the left and right legs use opposite
horizontal directions to generate rotational torque. The sideways gait uses the hip-abduction joints
to move the legs laterally. First, the robot tilts its body away from the direction of movement,
shifting its weight onto the supporting legs. The legs on the other side are then moved outward one
at a time. After that, the robot tilts its body in the opposite direction, allowing the remaining
legs to move inward one at a time. Repeating this sequence moves the robot sideways while three legs
support the body during each step.

The software architecture is organized into four functional layers. The lowest layer abstracts the
hardware interface, managing communication with the PWM driver and providing a unified interface for
setting servo angles. Above this sits the kinematics layer, which implements the lookup table and
interpolation logic to translate desired foot positions into joint angles. The gait generation layer
defines the periodic leg movement patterns, swing and stance trajectories, and orchestrates
their execution across multiple legs according to the selected gait pattern.
The highest layer comprises the application interfaces that allow human operators to command
the robot. The primary control interface is a web application, written in HTML and JavaScript,
connected to a web server, written in Python, which runs on the Raspberry Pi and exposes HTTP-based
remote-control endpoints. HTTP is a request-response application protocol operating over TCP, in
which the client (the operator's browser) initiates a connection and sends a request, and the server
returns a response~\parencite{fielding2022}. The server listens on a dedicated port and handles two
types of requests. A GET request to the root path returns the HTML control page, which contains a
JavaScript component that captures keyboard events. When the operator presses a key, the script
issues an asynchronous POST request to \texttt{/moveForward}, \texttt{/moveBackwards},
\texttt{/walkSidewaysLeft}, or \texttt{/walkSidewaysRight}. The server parses the request path to
determine the desired action and, because HTTP itself is stateless, maintains an internal state
machine to track whether the robot is idle, standing, or executing a gait. Upon receiving a movement
command, the server transitions to the moving state and spawns a background execution thread so that
the gait can run without blocking the communication interface. A timeout mechanism automatically
returns the robot to a resting position if no further commands are received for three seconds,
preventing the robot from remaining in an active stance indefinitely. This design prioritizes
responsiveness and simplicity, making the robot accessible to operators without requiring
specialized control hardware or software installation. For development and debugging, separate
command-line utilities, Python scripts, allow individual servos to be positioned manually or execute
different predefined gaits. Some tools were essential during calibration and mechanical assembly
because they enable direct control of any joint without starting the full gait system, the other
were useful for testing individual gaits. The calibration tools were also used to set the servos to
the angles required for collecting lookup-table measurements.

\subsection{Object detection model}
\label{subsec:object-detection-models}
Before discussing the YOLO architecture, it is important to understand the underlying technology
that makes it possible: the Convolutional Neural Network (CNN). A CNN is a class of deep neural
networks specifically designed for processing grid-structured data such as images. Unlike
traditional fully connected networks, where every neuron is connected to every neuron in the
adjacent layer, a CNN employs convolutional layers that apply learnable filters (kernels) across
local regions of the input. Each filter detects specific visual features of a given image,
such as edges, textures, shapes, or increasingly abstract patterns, by computing dot products
between the filter weights and the local pixel values. The output of this operation is a feature map
that preserves the spatial relationships within the image. CNNs can also incorporate pooling layers,
which progressively reduce the spatial dimensions of the feature maps, thereby decreasing
computational complexity and providing a degree of translation tolerance. This hierarchical
feature extraction, combined with weight sharing across spatial locations, makes CNNs exceptionally
effective for computer vision tasks while requiring far fewer parameters than fully connected
networks~\parencite{lecun2015}.

The original YOLO introduced an innovative approach to object detection~\parencite{yolo}. Before it,
systems such as the Deformable Parts Model (DPM)~\parencite{felzenszwalb2010} and Region-based
Convolutional Neural Network (R-CNN)~\parencite{rcnn} used classifiers in multi-stage detection
pipelines. Classifiers for each object were used to evaluate it at various locations and scales in
a test image, either in a sliding-window manner or on proposed bounding boxes. A post-processing
stage would then refine the bounding boxes and remove duplicate detections. Such cumbersome
pipelines were slow and difficult to optimize because of the heterogeneous nature of their
components. The new approach introduced by YOLO uses the whole image to train the model to predict
objects, converting object detection into a single regression problem. This method uses a single
convolutional network to predict multiple bounding boxes and their class probabilities
simultaneously, making it swift by simplifying the pipeline. Unlike the aforementioned mechanisms,
YOLO can reason globally by examining the whole image, which gives it the advantage of learning
generalized representations of objects~\parencite{yolo}.

The following description applies to the original YOLO model. The model starts by dividing the
input image into an $S \times S$ grid. Each grid cell is responsible for predicting bounding boxes,
their confidence scores, and class probabilities. Grid cells are not limited to one bounding box;
instead, they can predict $B$ boxes but only one set of $C$ conditional class probabilities per
cell. In the original paper, the authors use $B=2$ and $C=20$ on a $7 \times 7$ grid. Each bounding
box is made up of five numbers: the $x$ and $y$ positions, representing the location of the center
relative to the grid cell; the width and height relative to the entire image; and the confidence
score, representing the predicted Intersection over Union (IoU) between the bounding box and the
ground truth, multiplied by the probability that an object is present. Formally,
\begin{equation}
\text{confidence} = \Pr(\text{object}) \times \text{IoU}_{\text{pred}}^{\text{truth}}.
\end{equation}
If no object exists in the cell, the confidence score should ideally be zero. The class
probabilities are conditioned on the cell containing an object, denoted
$\Pr(\text{class}_i \mid \text{object})$. At test time, the class-specific confidence for each box
is obtained by multiplying the conditional class probabilities by the box confidence:
\begin{equation}
\Pr(\text{class}_i \mid \text{object}) \times \Pr(\text{object}) \times
\text{IoU}_{\text{pred}}^{\text{truth}} =
\Pr(\text{class}_i) \times \text{IoU}_{\text{pred}}^{\text{truth}}.
\end{equation}
This product encodes both the probability that the box contains an object of class $i$ and how well
the box fits the object~\parencite{yolo}.

The original network architecture borrows heavily from GoogLeNet, also known as
Inception-v1~\parencite{yolo}. It is made up of 24 convolutional layers followed by two fully connec
ted
layers. The convolutional layers handle feature extraction, starting with simple edges and textures
and building up to more complex patterns, while the fully connected layers map these features to
the final output tensor of size $S \times S \times (B \times 5 + C)$.

For training, the authors chose Sum-Squared Error (SSE) as the loss function. This single loss
covers three things at once: the accuracy of bounding box coordinates, the confidence score, and
the class prediction. A challenge here was that most grid cells in any image do not contain an
object, which can bias the model toward always predicting ``no object''. To prevent this, the loss
function applies different weights to different parts of the prediction. Coordinate errors are
multiplied by $\lambda_{\text{coord}} = 5$ to increase their importance, while confidence errors
for empty boxes are scaled down by $\lambda_{\text{noobj}} = 0.5$. This ensures that the network
pays more attention to getting the boxes right when objects are actually present~\parencite{yolo}.

Because every cell predicts multiple bounding boxes, the network naturally generates many
overlapping detections around the same object. To deal with this, YOLO applies non-maximum
suppression (NMS) as a post-processing step. NMS works by selecting the box with the highest
confidence and removing lower-confidence boxes that overlap with it significantly, meaning boxes
whose IoU exceeds a chosen threshold. The end result is usually a single high-confidence bounding
box per object rather than multiple overlapping predictions~\parencite{yolo}.

Since its original release, YOLO has evolved through several major architectural shifts. Early
iterations moved from pure regression to anchor-based detection, introduced batch normalization
and multi-scale training, and adopted deeper residual backbones with feature pyramid-style
predictions at multiple resolutions. The next wave of improvements focused on training efficiency
and deployment accessibility: Cross-Stage Partial networks reduced computational cost, the
ecosystem migrated from Darknet to native PyTorch, and automated augmentation and anchor
calculation removed much of the manual tuning. More recently, the architecture has shifted toward
anchor-free detection, decoupled classification and regression heads, and unified frameworks that
integrate object detection with segmentation and pose estimation under a single model family. For
this project, I decided to use the Ultralytics YOLO library with the YOLOv8n model
\parencite{ultralytics}. YOLOv8 uses an anchor-free detector and separate outputs for classification
and bounding-box regression. I chose the nano variant to reduce memory use and inference time on the
Raspberry Pi.

As mentioned before in the project requirements, the selected target classes are dogs and humans.
I chose two significant datasets in the history of object detection: COCO~\parencite{coco} and
Stanford Dogs~\parencite{stanforddogs}. COCO contains more than $330,000$ images, of which over
$200,000$ are labelled, and approximately $1.5$ million object instances across 80 object
categories. Stanford Dogs contains $20,580$ annotated images of dogs belonging to 120 breeds. Even
though the datasets are suitable as they are, they must be adapted to the case I have, which is a
close-to-ground quadruped robot. The average height of the camera in the standing position is
approximately $17$~cm, with an upward tilt of $15^\circ$.

To understand why adaptation is necessary, it helps to look at what the camera actually sees. The
camera has a horizontal field of view of $54^\circ$ and a vertical field of view of
$41^\circ$~\parencite{cameraV1}. The optical axis is tilted $15^\circ$ above horizontal, so the
vertical field of view is split around this axis: the top edge of the frame is
$15^\circ + 20.5^\circ = 35.5^\circ$ above horizontal, and the bottom edge is
$15^\circ - 20.5^\circ = -5.5^\circ$, i.e., $5.5^\circ$ below horizontal.

Expressing all distances in centimeters, the height of the top edge at horizontal distance $d$ is
\begin{equation}
y_{\text{top}} = 17 + d \cdot \tan(35.5^\circ) \approx 17 + 0.713d,
\end{equation}
and the height of the bottom edge is
\begin{equation}
y_{\text{bottom}} = 17 - d \cdot \tan(5.5^\circ) \approx 17 - 0.096d.
\end{equation}
The factor $0.713$ comes from $\tan(35.5^\circ)$ and the factor $0.096$ comes from
$\tan(5.5^\circ)$. The total vertical span of the frame at distance $d$ is the difference between
these two values, which works out to approximately $0.809d$~cm. Anything outside the band
$[y_{\text{bottom}}, y_{\text{top}}]$ is cut off.

The horizontal width of the view is determined by the $54^\circ$ horizontal field of view. With half
of that angle being $27^\circ$, the total width is
\begin{equation}
w = 2d \cdot \tan(27^\circ) \approx 1.019d.
\end{equation}

The table shows that the low position of the robot's camera does not capture full humans and dogs
at closer distances. The closer distances are important because those are the distances where dogs
and human can interact with the robot and the robot's vision must support detecting them. This is
a very different viewpoint from the standard dataset images, which are usually taken at eye level
and show complete objects. Additionally, the robot's camera may have lower image quality whereas
standard datasets usually contain higher resolution images. Training on those images without
adjustment would leave the model unprepared for such perspective or image quality.

To address these problems and satisfy the project requirements, I created a new custom-built dataset
that combines modified COCO and Stanford Dogs datasets. The two output classes are human and dog.
The split between training, validation, and test is $80\%$, $10\%$, and $10\%$ for each dataset.
Several checks were added to reduce incorrect labels and data leakage. Stanford Dogs provides dog
annotations but can also show people who are not annotated. A pretrained detector was therefore used
as a screening step, and $3,869$ Stanford images with a person detection at a confidence of at least
$0.20$ were excluded. Since COCO annotates both humans and dogs, such a detector was not needed in
that case.

The source images keep their native resolution and aspect ratio. They are not stretched or
permanently resized to the camera resolution, however, during training and testing, Ultralytics
scales each image to fit a $320 \times 320$ input and adds padding where necessary. This
letterboxing avoids distorting people and dogs. Partial examples are included only in the training
split. The preparation code creates one additional square view biased towards the lower part of a
randomly selected object, which results in approximately $25\%$ of positive training sources. The
crop aims to retain roughly from $40\%$ to $75\%$ of a human's height or from $60\%$ to $90\%$ of
a dog's height and the full source image always remains in the dataset. The remaining augmentation
applies small color changes, rotation up to $3^\circ$, translation up to $10\%$, scaling, and
horizontal flipping.

The model is trained on a MacBook Pro with a 10-core Apple M2 Pro chip, a 16-core GPU, and $16$~GB
of unified memory. The training utilizes the PyTorch Metal Performance Shaders (MPS)
backend, which is Apple's framework, built on the Metal graphics API, for accelerating machine
learning workloads on the GPU~\parencite{applemetal,pytorchmps}. Architecturally, MPS abstracts the
complexity of writing low-level Metal Shading Language by providing optimized operations and
kernels. These perform parallel mathematical operations such as multidimensional matrix
multiplication and convolution~\parencite{pytorchmps}.

The execution of these operations is orchestrated by PyTorch's dynamic computational graph, which
maps the sequence of mathematical operations and data dependencies required by the model. When
the graph is dispatched to the \texttt{mps} device, PyTorch sends supported operations to the
MPS backend for execution on the GPU~\parencite{pytorchmps}. Apple silicon uses a Unified Memory
Architecture (UMA), in which the CPU and GPU access a shared pool of physical
memory~\parencite{appleshared}. This can reduce the need to copy data between separate CPU and GPU
memory pools. PyTorch's MPS backend maps supported graph operations to MPS Graph functions or
optimized Metal kernels. The resulting GPU commands are encoded into Metal command buffers, which
are scheduled for execution on the GPU\@. This tight integration of Apple's hardware with PyTorch
allows the GPU to accelerate training compared with CPU-only execution.

\subsubsection{Training configuration}
The model is initialized from pretrained \texttt{yolov8n.pt} weights and fine-tuned on the prepared
dataset~\parencite{ultralytics}. The environment contains Python 3.11.13, PyTorch 2.12.0, and
Ultralytics 8.4.53.
Several training runs were performed using the Python notebook and the Ultralytics command-line
interface and both methods are equivalent. Here is the final command configuration I used for
training the model:
\begin{minted}{bash}
yolo detect train model=yolov8n.pt \
data=dataset.yaml \
imgsz=320 \
batch=16 \
epochs=60 \
device=mps \
workers=0 \
seed=42 \
deterministic=True \
val=True \
mosaic=1.0
\end{minted}
where:
\begin{description}
  \item[\texttt{yolo detect train}] The Ultralytics CLI command that selects the YOLO detection task
and starts a training run.
  \item[\texttt{model=yolov8n.pt}] The initial weights to load (YOLOv8 Nano). This uses a small,
fast pretrained model as a starting point.
  \item[\texttt{data}] The value \texttt{dataset.yaml} points to the dataset configuration YAML
describing training/validation image locations and class names.
  \item[\texttt{imgsz=320}] Sets the input canvas to $320 \times 320$ pixels. Images keep their
aspect ratio and are padded as needed. A smaller input reduces memory and computation but can
reduce accuracy for small objects.
  \item[\texttt{batch=16}] Number of images processed per optimization step. This value fit within
the available unified memory during the completed run.
  \item[\texttt{epochs=60}] Number of full passes over the training set.
  \item[\texttt{device=mps}] Use the Apple Metal Performance Shaders (MPS) GPU backend on macOS
instead of the CPU.
  \item[\texttt{workers=0}] Load data in the main process rather than in worker subprocesses.
  \item[\texttt{seed=42}] Fix the random seed to ensure reproducibility of the training process.
  \item[\texttt{deterministic=True}] Forces deterministic algorithm use to improve reproducibility.
  \item[\texttt{val=True}] Evaluate the validation split after every epoch.
  \item[\texttt{mosaic=1.0}] Use mosaic augmentation during training.
\end{description}

Training the model for all 60 epochs took approximately 6 hours and 56 minutes. The best validation
result was obtained at the final epoch. The measured validation results are presented in
Chapter~\ref{chap:results}.

After training, the PyTorch checkpoint was exported to the NCNN format for deployment on the
Raspberry Pi because NCNN is designed for efficient execution on ARM-based
devices~\parencite{ncnn}. The export uses a batch size of one, a $320 \times 320$ input, and 32-bit
floating-point weights. It contains the same classes \texttt{human} and \texttt{dog} and produces
the NCNN parameter and binary weight files required for inference.

\section{Robot construction and locomotion results}
The robot was designed in Onshape to be easy to assemble with most of the parts printed in place.
Then, the printed parts with purchased components were assembled together. After that, the software
for the robot was developed using the Python programming language and the Adafruit ServoKit
library~\parencite{servokit}. The last steps were to prepare the dataset, train the YOLOv8n model,
and deploy it on the Raspberry Pi.

The mechanical result is an assembled quadruped robot with 12 actuated joints. Printing the body,
four legs, and eight servo holders took approximately 20.5 hours and used 490 grams of filament.
Based on the assembly process, the robot can be assembled in approximately one hour after the
printed parts are prepared. The final robot has the following measurements: the body is $30.5$~cm
long, the total length with legs is $38.5$~cm, the body width is $11.5$~cm, the total width with
legs is $20.5$~cm, the height when sitting is $8.8$~cm, and the height when standing is $17.3$~cm.
The total weight of the robot is $1,171$~g. The prototype was tested using a laboratory power supply
set to 6 V with a 6 A current limit.

The software can move the robot between various positions. Types of gaits implemented in the
software are crawl, trot, turning, and sideways movement gaits. The leg movement is calculated
using the lookup table, which converts the desired foot positions into hip and knee servo angles
through inverse distance weighting. The web interface provides forward, backward, and sideways
commands together with live video streaming and object detection.

Table~\ref{tab:locomotion-measurement} shows the measured locomotion result of the robot. I used
a script that makes the robot stand up and walk forward using the trot gait for 5 steps. By
recording a video of the robot and measuring the distance traveled with a ruler and the time taken,
I calculated the average speed of the robot with the following parameters. The measured speed is
$4.75$~cm/s ($0.0475$~m/s). \texttt{x\_start} and \texttt{x\_end} are the starting and ending
positions of each step relative to each leg's center position. \texttt{y\_ground} is the ground
position, i.e. the initial height of the foot when it is on the ground. \texttt{lift} is the height
of the foot when it is lifted during a step. \texttt{samples} is the number of samples used to

\section{Object detection results}
The YOLOv8n model completed 60 epochs. Table~\ref{tab:vision-validation-results} shows the aggregate
results from the validation split at the final and best epoch. Precision describes the fraction of
reported detections that are correct, while recall describes the fraction of annotated objects that
were found. mAP@50 uses an Intersection over Union threshold of $0.50$. mAP@50:95 averages the
result over thresholds from $0.50$ to $0.95$ and is therefore stricter about bounding-box
placement.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\hline
\textbf{Result} & \textbf{Precision} & \textbf{Recall} & \textbf{mAP@50} &
\textbf{mAP@50:95} \\
\hline
All classes & $85.17\%$ & $74.30\%$ & $80.26\%$ & $61.32\%$ \\
Human & $83.26\%$ & $52.59\%$ & $64.05\%$ & $39.88\%$ \\
Dog & $87.09\%$ & $96.01\%$ & $96.47\%$ & $82.77\%$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{Aggregate and per-class validation metrics for the 60-epoch YOLOv8n model.}
\label{tab:vision-validation-results}
\end{table}

The difference between mAP@50 and mAP@50:95 shows that accurate box placement at stricter overlap
thresholds remains more difficult. Recall is also lower than precision, which means that missed
objects remain an important weakness. The per-class results show that dog detection performs much
better than human detection. In particular, human recall is $52.59\%$, while dog recall is
$96.01\%$. This means that missed humans are the main weakness of the model. At the last epoch, the
training box, classification, and Distribution Focal Loss values were $0.749$, $0.545$, and
$1.007$. The corresponding validation values were $0.985$, $0.683$, and $1.020$.

\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}
This thesis presents the design and implementation of a low-cost quadruped robot with computer
vision. All the software is written in Python. The robot supports external control and live video
streaming. The resulting computer-vision model is a two-class YOLOv8n model converted to NCNN for
speed and efficiency. The dataset for the model keeps full scenes and includes partial training
views that approximate the visibility expected from the robot's low viewpoint.

The validation results show that the lightweight model can detect humans and dogs at a
$320 \times 320$ input resolution. However, the lower recall and the difference between mAP@50 and
mAP@50:95 show that missed objects and precise localization can still be improved.

The conducted outdoor test demonstrates that the model is capable of detecting humans and dogs in
real environments.

\chapter{Future work}
\label{chap:future-work}
The robot can be improved in several ways. Servo motors can be replaced with higher torque servos
to increase the robot's load capacity. Additionally, the robot can be equipped with a battery pack,
which was initially purchased but not installed, and a charging circuit to make it independent from
an external power supply. Also, the robot can be equipped with sensors, such as an IMU, to improve
its stability and navigation capabilities, or ground-contact sensors to detect the terrain and
adjust its gait accordingly. Moreover, shaft encoders can be added to the servos, or servos with
built-in encoders of such type can be used to provide feedback on their position and speed and help
automate the calibration process. Finally, training a DLR model in a simulated environment to
replace hardcoded gaits which would allow the robot to adapt to different terrains and obstacles.

The whole design can be scaled up to a larger robot with more powerful motors and a higher payload
capacity that would make it more suitable for practical applications.
